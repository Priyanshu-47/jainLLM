Executive Summary 

 

Publicly available evidence strongly indicates that Hindu and Jain religious texts, manuscript artifacts, temple imagery, iconography, and educational material are already being used in AI systems. The use is not confined to general web search: there are religion-specific retrieval systems, public fine-tuned models, OCR models trained on Sanskrit and Vedic manuscript imagery, Hugging Face datasets for Jain and Hindu texts, and computer-vision datasets involving deity images and temple architecture. 

 

The clearest cases are JainGPT and JainQQ for Jain textual search and question answering; AI-Yashvi as an inspectable Jain retrieval-augmented chatbot; Bhagavad Gītā-specific models such as GitaYukti-8B and Gita-Gemma-270M; and OCR models trained on Sanskrit or Vedic manuscript images. There are also repositories such as Jain Literature Open Repository, jainism-portal/jainaagam, and Viraasat AI that make religious texts or heritage-oriented AI workflows available for inspection and reuse. 

 

However, the evidence does not show that Hinduism or Jainism as complete, internally diverse religious traditions have been comprehensively trained into a single authoritative AI model. Most projects are narrower: RAG systems built on general-purpose language models, small supervised fine-tunes, embedding models, OCR/transcription tools, or machine-readable datasets. Their quality and legitimacy depend heavily on the source corpus, translation quality, licensing, citations, sectarian coverage, and expert review. 

 

The Claim 

 

The claim being checked is: 

 

> “There are specific platforms, GitHub repositories, datasets, or AI models that have been trained on, retrieve from, or otherwise use Hindu and Jain religious texts, artifacts, manuscripts, learning materials, temple architecture, or iconography.” 

 

This report evaluates whether public evidence supports that claim, and distinguishes between several very different kinds of AI use: 

 

1. Models directly fine-tuned on religious text; 

2. Retrieval-augmented generation systems that search religious corpora; 

3. OCR and vision models trained on manuscript or artifact images; 

4. Datasets that make Hindu or Jain materials AI-ready; 

5. Heritage and art-analysis systems involving temples, deities, diagrams, or manuscripts. 

 

Evidence For 

 

JainGPT and JainQQ — Jain religious question answering and semantic retrieval 

Finding: Strong evidence of active use of Jain literature in an AI answer-and-search system. 

Strength: Strong. 

 

JainGPT is a Jain-focused AI assistant associated with Jain eLibrary. Its public description says that it answers questions about Jain philosophy, scriptures, practices, and related learning using source material from the Jain eLibrary corpus. The service is designed to provide citations so users can inspect the underlying books, manuscripts, and commentaries. 

 

Reporting by Religion Unplugged and commentary from Swadharma identify a wider ecosystem that includes JainQQ, semantic search, OCR-derived textual materials, vector embeddings, and plans to process very large quantities of scanned Jain-library material. This is significant because Jain literature is spread across Prakrit, Sanskrit, Gujarati, Hindi, English, and other languages, often in scanned or difficult-to-search forms. 

 

The evidence suggests JainGPT is primarily a retrieval-augmented generation system rather than a wholly new Jain foundation model. In practical terms, that means it likely uses a general-purpose language model but grounds answers in retrieved Jain material. Such a design is useful because it can potentially show sources and reduce unsupported answers. It is still not equivalent to expert religious interpretation. 

 

AI-Yashvi GitHub repository — inspectable Jain RAG chatbot 

Finding: A public GitHub project directly implements Jain-oriented AI question answering. 

Strength: Strong. 

 

The GitHub repository saumyasanghvi03/AI-Yashvi is an open-source Jain-focused chatbot. It is described as a Streamlit-based RAG application capable of answering Jain spiritual and philosophical questions in English and Gujarati. 

 

Its listed capabilities include source-document ingestion, contextual retrieval, language detection, caching, content moderation, and fallback model support. It reportedly accepts formats including .txt, .md, .json, and .yaml, and references Jain knowledge sources such as Jain eLibrary, Jainpedia, JainWorld, Digital Jain Pathshala, and HereNow4U. 

 

The project is especially important because it is inspectable. Unlike a closed commercial chatbot, researchers can examine its code, source-handling approach, model configuration, and citation mechanisms. The repository reportedly uses general-purpose model families such as Qwen, Gemma, DialoGPT, and DistilGPT2. Therefore, it demonstrates Jain materials being used in AI, but it should not be described as proof that a new Jain-specific foundational model was trained from scratch. 

 

Jain Literature Open Repository — Jain textual corpus preparation and AI-generated translations 

Finding: Public repository organizing Jain texts for searchable and AI-relevant use. 

Strength: Strong for AI-assisted textual access; moderate for model training. 

 

The Jain Literature Open Repository (JLOR) is a GitHub initiative intended to organize Jain Āgamas, Anuyogas, texts associated with major Ācāryas, philosophical writings, English translations, and explanatory materials. 

 

The JLOR README explicitly notes that some translations are interim AI-generated translations, including work produced using Gemini 1.5 Flash and Claude Haiku. The project also states an intention to improve and verify translations through qualified Pandits, Upādhyāyas, Sādhus, and other traditional or scholarly reviewers. 

 

This is direct evidence of AI being used to expand access to Jain literature. It matters because many Jain texts remain inaccessible to modern readers due to script, language, digitization, and translation barriers. Yet the project’s own disclosure is also important: AI-generated translations are provisional and should not be confused with authoritative editions or traditional commentaries. 

 

The repository is marked NOASSERTION for licensing. This weakens any claim that the corpus is automatically available for unrestricted commercial use or large-scale model training. Availability on GitHub does not itself create a training license. 

 

Jain Āgam digital project — searchable Jain scripture infrastructure 

Finding: GitHub project for digitizing and structuring Jain Āgama literature. 

Strength: Moderate. 

 

The repository jainism-portal/jainaagam seeks to make Jain Āgama literature searchable, editable, multilingual, and Unicode-accessible. Its stated scope includes the 45 Jain Āgamas, original sūtras, explanations, meanings, and versions in multiple languages. 

 

This project is evidence of AI-relevant religious-text infrastructure. Searchable Unicode texts are much easier to use for semantic search, embeddings, RAG, translation alignment, OCR correction, and future model evaluation. However, it is not itself proof that a complete AI model has already been trained on all 45 Āgamas. 

 

JainBooks, Ātma Siddhi, and Jain philosophy datasets — public AI-ready Jain corpora 

Finding: Jain textual datasets are available on Hugging Face for AI research and applications. 

Strength: Strong. 

 

The Hugging Face dataset shethjenil/JainBooks reportedly contains approximately 32,400 rows of Jain literary material involving Sanskrit, Prakrit, Gujarati, and related traditions. Its examples include Jain scriptural and literary works such as Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra. A corpus of this scale can support semantic search, RAG, corpus analysis, transliteration, OCR cleanup, and experimental fine-tuning. 

 

The dataset HappyAIUser/Atma3.1-ShareGPT contains approximately 7,700 rows of English annotations based on Ātma Siddhi Śāstra. It includes literal translations, interpretive summaries, and contextual explanations. It is potentially useful for educational question answering and instruction fine-tuning, particularly because it has an Apache 2.0 license. 

 

A more focused resource, dataspoof/Jains, provides roughly 699 verse-level rows from Haribhadrasūri’s Śāstravārttāsamuccaya, including IAST Sanskrit transliteration, optional Devanāgarī, and verse identifiers. 

 

Together, these datasets establish that Jain text has been structured for AI use. They do not establish complete sectarian coverage. Jain traditions differ substantially across Śvetāmbara and Digambara contexts, canonical and post-canonical literature, languages, and lineages of commentary. 

 

Bhagavad Gītā-specific embedding model — Gita Assistant 

Finding: A public embedding model was explicitly fine-tuned for Bhagavad Gītā retrieval. 

Strength: Very strong. 

 

The Hugging Face project Shubhagaman/Gita-assistant is particularly clear evidence of direct AI adaptation to a Hindu religious text. It covers the Bhagavad Gītā’s 18 chapters and approximately 700 verses and is designed for multilingual retrieval across English, Hindi, and Sanskrit-oriented content. 

 

Its documentation identifies sentence-transformers/paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2 as the base model, reports 384-dimensional embeddings, and states that it was fine-tuned using Multiple Negatives Ranking Loss on English and Hindi Gītā translations. It is released under an MIT license. 

 

This is not a general religious chatbot; it is a specialized retrieval component. That specialization is valuable because scripture search benefits from verse-level retrieval and citation. It also illustrates a safer architecture than unrestricted answer generation: a system can retrieve relevant verses before drafting an explanation. 

 

GitaYukti-8B — 8-billion-parameter Bhagavad Gītā fine-tuned model 

Finding: Public model card claims an 8B language model was fine-tuned on curated Gītā material. 

Strength: Strong, subject to independent validation. 

 

ananddey/GitaYukti-8B is described as a model based on Qwen3-8B and fine-tuned on Bhagavad Gītā content. The model card reports approximately 6.7 million training tokens, including Sanskrit text, Hindi transliteration or translation, and English translations. It states that supervised fine-tuning was performed using NVIDIA H100 GPUs and F16 precision. 

 

This is among the strongest examples of a Hindu text being used directly for language-model fine-tuning. The model is intended to discuss karma, dharma, mokṣa, yoga, ethical conflict, duty, and spiritual reflection. 

 

Yet the model’s scope must not be exaggerated. The Bhagavad Gītā is central to many Hindu traditions, but it is not equivalent to Hinduism as a whole. Its interpretation differs across Advaita, Viśiṣṭādvaita, Dvaita, Śaiva, Śākta, Smārta, Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇava, reformist, and modern secular readings. 

 

Gita-Gemma-270M and Bhagavad Gītā Guidance QA 

Finding: Smaller Gītā-oriented instructional datasets and fine-tuned models are publicly available. 

Strength: Strong. 

 

The model abagade/gemma-3-270m-bhagavad-gita-v1 is a lightweight Gemma-based model of about 270 million parameters. Its card describes LoRA/QLoRA fine-tuning using roughly 700 question-answer pairs, Sanskrit ślokas, English translations, and simplified explanations. 

 

The related dataset abagade/bhagavad-gita-guidance-qa also contains approximately 700 instruction-style examples linking contemporary questions with a verse, citation, translation, and modern-life explanation. 

 

These resources show a practical pattern: scripture is being reformatted into conversational AI data. However, the dataset documentation reportedly acknowledges that prompts and explanations are synthetic or AI-generated. Therefore, the verse material may be scriptural, while the surrounding interpretation is modern and machine-generated rather than traditional commentary. 

 

Vedic, Purāṇic, and broader classical-language datasets 

Finding: Hindu textual corpora have been made available in machine-readable form for AI applications. 

Strength: Strong for data availability; moderate for evidence of actual training. 

 

The dataset shinigamiRaj/IndianVedasOriginal includes material associated with the Ṛgveda, Sāmaveda, Yajurveda, Atharvaveda, Sanskrit verses, Hindi and English translations, and traditional knowledge texts such as the Caraka Saṃhitā and Suśruta Saṃhitā. 

 

Similarly, dataspoof/Puranas-dataset offers machine-readable Sanskrit material from a range of Purāṇic sources, including selections associated with the Agni Purāṇa, Bhāgavata Purāṇa, Devī Bhāgavata Purāṇa, Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa, Śiva Purāṇa, Skanda Purāṇa, Viṣṇu Purāṇa, and others. 

 

The broader RSL-SHRUTI Sangraha dataset reportedly contains about 1.19 million lines and 718 MB of Sanskrit and multiple Indian languages. It includes OCR cleaning, script normalization, duplicate removal, and passage-boundary processing, under a CC BY-NC 4.0 license. 

 

These datasets are important because AI systems require structured, machine-readable text. They demonstrate preparation for AI use, although the mere presence of a dataset does not prove that a particular public model has already trained on every text it contains. 

 

Sanskrit and Vedic manuscript OCR models 

Finding: Hindu manuscript imagery has explicitly been used to train OCR and vision-language models. 

Strength: Very strong. 

 

The model Piyush3142/trocr-sanskrit-ocr is based on microsoft/trocr-base-printed and is reportedly fine-tuned using approximately 11,700 samples from the yzk/veda-ocr-ms dataset. Its stated split includes about 10,560 training samples and 1,174 test samples. This is direct evidence that Vedic or Sanskrit manuscript-image material was used to fine-tune an AI transcription model. 

 

The vision-language model diabolic6045/Sanskrit-Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct-OCR is described as a LoRA fine-tune of Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct for Sanskrit image-to-text OCR. Its model card reports evaluation across 314 samples, with 86.38% character-level accuracy, 50.44% token-level Jaccard similarity, and only 1.59% exact-match accuracy. 

 

Those numbers reveal both achievement and limitation. The system can process manuscript-like Sanskrit images, but the extremely low exact-match rate means it is not reliable enough for unsupervised publication of religious texts. A single OCR error can alter a mantra, grammatical inflection, name, verse reference, ritual instruction, or philosophical term. 

 

Oxford Hindu manuscript AI project 

Finding: Academic project directly applies AI handwriting and text recognition to Hindu manuscript artifacts. 

Strength: Strong. 

 

The University of Oxford project, “Digital Humanities in Hindu Studies: Creating AI Models for Handwriting and Text Recognition in South Asian Manuscripts”, is direct institutional evidence of AI use on Hindu manuscript heritage. 

 

The project focuses on historical South Asian manuscript materials, including palm-leaf manuscripts and materials related to Śākta and Vaiṣṇava traditions. It uses historical Devanāgarī OCR, handwriting recognition, Transkribus workflows, digital curation, and searchable transcription development. 

 

Its goal is preservation, accessibility, editing, and scholarship rather than automated theological judgment. This distinction is important: AI can help scholars read and catalog difficult manuscripts without replacing philology, palaeography, religious expertise, or community interpretation. 

 

Jain manuscripts and religious-art collections on OPenn 

Finding: Digitized Jain artifacts with comparatively clear access conditions can support AI research. 

Strength: Strong for source availability; moderate for evidence of completed training. 

 

The University of Pennsylvania OPenn platform provides Jain manuscript materials with technical access and relatively clear rights information. Ms. Indic 20, a collection of Jain scripture and devotional materials, is available under CC BY 4.0 with public-domain treatment where applicable. 

 

Other particularly relevant holdings include Ms. Indic 25, an illustrated Jain Sanskrit manuscript with diagrams and tabular structures; Ms. Indic 9, an eighteenth-century Saṅghayaṇasūtra manuscript containing Jain cosmological diagrams and Mughal-influenced Jain iconography; and Ms. Indic 15, containing citrabandha picture poems, yantras, mantras, and devotional content. 

 

These are valuable for OCR, layout segmentation, diagram recognition, manuscript analysis, iconographic research, and multimodal AI. They are especially important because most AI research on Indian religious heritage has concentrated on text; these collections show that visual and material religious culture can also become AI-relevant data. 

 

Deity-image and temple-heritage AI datasets 

Finding: Hindu and Jain religious imagery and temple architecture are being used in computer-vision research. 

Strength: Moderate to strong. 

 

The Yegiiii/deities-25 dataset reportedly contains 8,239 images across 25 deity classes, including Hindu and Jain deity imagery. It has 6,583 training images and 1,656 validation images. This is direct evidence that Indic religious imagery has been assembled for image-classification experiments. 

 

The dataset’s limitations are serious: it reportedly includes social-media images, smartphone photographs, and diffusion-generated imagery. This means it should not be treated as authoritative iconographic data. It may have mislabeling, regional bias, unclear copyright, ambiguous images, and blurred distinctions between Hindu and Jain traditions. 

 

For temple heritage, rxhxn2904/arqgan-indian-temple-restoration-dataset, linked to Viraasat AI, reportedly contains about 27,000 rows and 42.5 GB of data derived from Indian temple architecture workflows. It uses 3D point clouds, segmentation labels, mesh views, synthetic damage, and restoration targets. KoyilNet, under CC BY 4.0, is another temple architecture dataset intended for recognition, preservation, educational research, and style analysis. 

 

These projects show AI use for cultural heritage and architecture, but synthetic restoration outputs should never be mistaken for historical evidence of a temple’s original condition. 

 

Evidence Against 

 

Lack of proof of a comprehensive Hindu or Jain foundation model 

Finding: No cited source establishes a fully documented, comprehensive, authoritative Hindu or Jain religion model. 

Strength: Strong. 

 

The evidence supports many narrow systems, but not a single model trained on all Hindu or Jain traditions. Hinduism encompasses Vedic, Vedāntic, Śaiva, Śākta, Vaiṣṇava, Smārta, regional, devotional, philosophical, ritual, and modern traditions. Jainism includes major Śvetāmbara and Digambara traditions, canonical Āgamas, later commentaries, philosophical works, ritual manuals, biographies, and regional literature. 

 

A Bhagavad Gītā model can be a useful Gītā assistant, but it cannot accurately represent Hinduism in full. Likewise, a Jain RAG chatbot based on Jain eLibrary texts may be useful without representing every Jain sect, language, commentary tradition, or authority. 

 

Incomplete technical disclosure from religion-specific platforms 

Finding: JainGPT’s public documentation does not fully disclose its base model, training corpus, ranking method, or evaluation process. 

Strength: Strong. 

 

JainGPT is meaningful evidence of Jain content being used in AI. However, public information does not provide a complete technical audit trail: the underlying base model, full indexed corpus, vector database, source selection process, citation ranking, evaluation methodology, sectarian coverage, and scholar-review workflow are not fully public. 

 

This does not disprove its usefulness. It means outside researchers cannot independently verify all claims about coverage, accuracy, representativeness, or training. 

 

Dataset availability does not prove training rights or ethical permission 

Finding: Public hosting on GitHub, Hugging Face, or Zenodo does not automatically authorize unrestricted AI training. 

Strength: Strong. 

 

Several datasets draw partly on GRETIL or other historical-text sources. Rights can differ between an old manuscript, a modern transcription, a translation, a scholarly annotation, a scan, metadata, and derivative AI-generated text. 

 

For example, JLOR’s NOASSERTION license status means users should not assume bulk scraping, commercial reuse, redistribution, or model training is permitted. Likewise, Vitrag eLibrary’s manuscript materials are valuable but should not automatically be considered an open bulk-training corpus merely because they can be viewed online. 

 

OCR accuracy remains insufficient for unreviewed sacred-text publication 

Finding: OCR performance data show that manuscript transcription remains error-prone. 

Strength: Strong. 

 

The reported 1.59% exact-match accuracy for Sanskrit-Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct-OCR is an important caution. Even an 86.38% character-level accuracy score can conceal substantial problems in religious texts, because textual accuracy is not merely statistical. One changed vowel, consonant, diacritic, case ending, name, or verse number can alter meaning. 

 

AI OCR is therefore best used for draft transcription, search indexing, and scholarly assistance—not for replacing human verification of sacred manuscripts. 

 

Verdict: Supported 

 

The central claim is Supported. 

 

There are identifiable platforms, GitHub repositories, Hugging Face datasets, OCR models, fine-tuned language models, and heritage-AI projects that use Hindu and Jain texts, artifacts, images, manuscripts, architecture, and learning materials. 

 

The strongest confirmed examples include: 

 

• JainGPT and JainQQ for Jain retrieval and answer generation; 

• AI-Yashvi for Jain RAG-based chat; 

• JLOR and jainaagam for Jain textual infrastructure; 

• Gita Assistant for Gītā-specific embedding retrieval; 

• GitaYukti-8B and Gita-Gemma-270M for Gītā-oriented fine-tuning; 

• Piyush3142/trocr-sanskrit-ocr for Vedic/Sanskrit manuscript OCR; 

• Oxford’s Hindu manuscript handwriting-recognition project; 

• OPenn Jain manuscript collections for OCR, diagram, manuscript-layout, and visual-heritage research; 

• Viraasat AI and related temple-restoration datasets. 

 

Nuance & Caveats 

 

The most important distinction is between training, retrieval, and data availability. A Gītā-specific fine-tuned model is direct model training. JainGPT and AI-Yashvi appear more closely aligned with retrieval-augmented generation, where a general model consults a Jain corpus before answering. OPenn manuscripts and temple datasets are source material that can support AI, but their availability does not prove that a particular model has been trained on them. 

 

Religious authority is another major concern. AI systems can retrieve verses, summarize translations, transcribe manuscript images, and assist learners. They should not be assumed to resolve doctrinal disputes, identify correct ritual practice, provide authoritative spiritual counsel, or represent all Hindu or Jain traditions neutrally. 

 

Finally, researchers and developers should adopt strong safeguards: disclose sources and licenses; distinguish original text from translation; cite chapter and verse; identify sectarian context; label AI-generated translations; preserve provenance for manuscript and artifact images; and involve qualified scholars and community representatives. These practices are especially important where sacred texts, images, diagrams, temple heritage, and living religious traditions are turned into machine-readable data.
